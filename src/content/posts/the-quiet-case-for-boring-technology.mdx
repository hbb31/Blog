Every few years I go through the same ritual. A new tool appears, it promises to fix the friction in my day, and I spend a weekend moving my notes, tasks or code into it. A few months later, I quietly move most of it back. The tools that survive this cycle are almost always the dull ones: plain text, a calendar, a shell script I wrote in an afternoon and haven't touched since.

This essay is an attempt to explain why that happens, and why I've stopped fighting it.

## What "boring" actually means

Boring doesn't mean old, and it doesn't mean bad. A boring tool is one whose failure modes you already understand. When it breaks, you know roughly why, and you know roughly how to fix it. That knowledge is the real asset, and it takes years to accumulate.

New tools reset that clock. Each one arrives with a fresh set of unknowns: how it syncs, how it handles conflicts, what happens when the company behind it changes direction. You can learn all of that, but learning it has a cost that rarely shows up in the launch post.

## The hidden budget of attention

I think of my attention as a budget, and every tool I depend on takes a small, permanent slice of it.[^1] A tool that "just works" takes a tiny slice. A tool that needs updates, migrations and workarounds takes a much bigger one, even on the days I'm not actively fighting it.

When I list the tools I've used for more than five years, the list is short and unglamorous. It is also the list of things I never think about. That isn't a coincidence.

## A small, practical test

Before I adopt something new now, I ask three questions:

1. Can I get my data out in a format I can read without this tool?
2. Will it still work if the company behind it disappears tomorrow?
3. Does it solve a problem I have _today_, or one I might have later?

<Callout tone="tip" title="Try this">
Write the three questions on a sticky note next to your monitor. The next time a launch post tempts you, answer them honestly before you sign up.
</Callout>

If the answers are "yes, yes, today", I'll give it a real trial. Otherwise I wait. Waiting costs almost nothing, and most tools that matter are still around a year later, only more stable.

## Where new tools do earn their place

None of this is an argument against progress. Sometimes a new tool removes a whole category of work, and refusing to adopt it would be stubbornness, not wisdom. Version control was once new. So were search engines.

The difference is in the size of the improvement. A tool that is 10% nicer rarely pays back the cost of switching. A tool that changes what is possible usually does.

## Living with boring choices

Choosing boring technology feels like missing out, at least at first. Then the feeling fades, and what's left is time: time not spent migrating, not spent reading changelogs, not spent rebuilding a system that was fine.

## Conclusion
